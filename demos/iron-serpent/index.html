🐍 Iron Serpent

Serpent-256-CTR · Argon2id · HMAC-SHA256 · encrypt-then-MAC

Encrypt and decrypt with a passphrase-derived key, then watch the block cipher's diffusion, its CTR keystream, and its security margin come apart under the hood.

WHY IT MATTERS

Real secrecy needs more than a strong cipher: a weak passphrase, a reused nonce, or a missing MAC each breaks the whole scheme. Seeing these pieces work together is how you learn to trust — or distrust — an encryption tool.

Encrypt

What happens inside Encrypt (and where each output field comes from)
  1. Passphrase → the secret you type. Never stored, never sent.
  2. Argon2id(passphrase, salt) → a fresh random salt is drawn, then Argon2id (64 MiB, 3 passes) slowly stretches the passphrase into a 256-bit masterKey. The salt is saved so decryption can repeat this step.
  3. HKDF(masterKey) → splits the one masterKey into two independent keys — an encKey for the cipher and a separate macKey for the tag. Different jobs get different keys.
  4. Serpent-256-CTR(encKey, nonce) → a fresh random nonce seeds the counter; Serpent encrypts the counter to a keystream, XORed with your plaintext to make the ciphertext.
  5. HMAC-SHA256(macKey, salt‖nonce‖ciphertext) → authenticates everything above into the mac tag. Encrypt-then-MAC: the tag is computed over the ciphertext, so decryption can verify it before running Serpent.

The JSON output packs exactly these results: salt, nonce, ciphertext, and mac — everything decryption needs except your passphrase.

Why two keys? HKDF and key separation

Argon2id gives us one 256-bit masterKey. But the cipher and the MAC must never share a key — reusing one key for two different algorithms can let one leak the other. So HKDF fans that single masterKey out into two independent subkeys:

masterKey256-bit, from Argon2id → HKDF “enc” → → HKDF “mac” → encKeySerpent-256-CTR macKeyHMAC-SHA256

What if we reused one key for both? Encrypting and authenticating with the same key ties the two constructions together, and a weakness in how they interact can expose the key or forge tags. HKDF makes them cryptographically independent — the same reason you never reuse a password across accounts.

Argon2id Parameters
  • Time cost: 3 iterations
  • Memory cost: 65,536 KiB (64 MiB)
  • Parallelism: 1
  • Output: 32 bytes (256-bit key)
  • Salt: 16 bytes (random per operation)

The HMAC is verified before Serpent ever runs. If one bit of the ciphertext or the tag changes, verification fails and decryption is refused — this is why we verify-then-decrypt (encrypt-then-MAC).

Inside Serpent: the 32 rounds, step by step

The buttons above say "full 32 rounds," but those rounds have been a black box — until now. Serpent turns plaintext into ciphertext by repeating one recipe 32 times: mix in a round key → substitute every 4-bit group through an S-box (confusion) → shuffle the bits with a linear transform (diffusion). Step through one 128-bit block below and watch a single flipped input bit avalanche across the whole block. Security Margin Visualization

How to read this — and how NOT to. The coloured prefix on each bar is the deepest round count that any published academic result has reached on a reduced-round version of the cipher. It is not a countdown to being broken. A reduced-round distinguisher on 12 of Serpent's 32 rounds does not mean the full cipher is "37% cracked" — every cipher shown here is unbroken at its full round count. The green remainder is the security margin: rounds that no public attack comes near. A wider margin is a hedge against future analysis, not a measure of how close today's cipher is to failing.

Avalanche Effect

Strict Avalanche Criterion: a single-bit change to the input of a strong block cipher should flip about half of the output bits. Encrypt one 128-bit block below, flip a single input bit, and watch how many of Serpent-256's 128 output bits change. A result near 50% is exactly what good diffusion looks like.
Why a public key here? This panel uses one fixed, published demo key — secrecy isn't the point. CTR Mode: Block Cipher → Stream Cipher

How does a cipher that can only encrypt 16-byte blocks handle a message of any length? In CTR (Counter) mode, Serpent never encrypts your message at all. It encrypts a counter block — the nonce, then nonce+1, then nonce+2… — to produce a keystream, and the plaintext is simply XORed against it. Edit the message or nonce below and watch the keystream change. This is the exact construction the Encrypt panel above uses.

About Serpent

The Designers

Affiliations below are the ones printed on the 1998 AES submission paper. That paper is jointly authored and does not apportion individual contributions — who designed the S-boxes, the key schedule or the overall structure is not stated.

  • Ross Anderson — Cambridge University, England. Security engineering researcher.
  • Eli Biham — Technion, Haifa, Israel. Co-inventor of differential cryptanalysis (with Adi Shamir).
  • Lars Knudsen — University of Bergen, Norway (he moved to the Technical University of Denmark around 2001). Known for integral and impossible differential cryptanalysis.

Israeli Cryptographic Lineage

Eli Biham studied under Adi Shamir (the "S" in RSA) at the Weizmann Institute. Together they developed differential cryptanalysis — the first published general technique that could attack a block cipher faster than brute force in principle. It did not break DES. Their attack on the full 16 rounds needs roughly 247 chosen plaintexts, which is worse than exhaustive search in practice; DES's S-boxes turned out to have been designed to resist it, IBM having known the method internally as "the T attack" back in 1974. DES was broken in practice by exhaustive key search against its 56-bit key (the EFF's Deep Crack machine, 1998). What differential cryptanalysis did change is how every block cipher since has been designed and argued for. Biham brought that understanding of attack mechanisms to Serpent, building a cipher with an explicit resistance argument against the techniques he himself had pioneered.

AES Competition Outcome

In the AES competition (1997–2000), Serpent placed second to Rijndael. Rijndael received 86 votes to Serpent's 59. NIST's selection criteria weighted performance alongside security. Rijndael was faster with fewer rounds (10/12/14 vs 32), while Serpent prioritized a conservative security margin — using twice as many rounds as needed to block all known shortcut attacks at the time.
